# Distributed Engineering & Energy Support Network

## 

---

# Executive Summary

This initiative is focused on building a distributed civilian engineering and repair network to support frontline drone and energy operations in Ukraine.

The current battlefield is heavily dependent on drones, mobile energy systems, communications equipment, and rapid field repairs. However, qualified military engineers are extremely limited and have become one of the primary operational bottlenecks.

The goal of this project is to offload simple but critical engineering, assembly, repair, and energy infrastructure tasks from frontline specialists to a structured support network operating in safer environments.

This creates:

- faster turnaround times;
- scalable repair capacity;
- lower operational costs;
- reusable energy infrastructure;
- resilient supply chains;
- sustainable long-term support systems.

---

# Current Operational Problem

## Engineering Personnel Are Overloaded

Modern warfare has become highly dependent on:

- drone reconnaissance;
- FPV systems;
- satellite communications;
- portable energy infrastructure;
- field charging stations;
- improvised technical solutions.

At the same time, experienced engineers are extremely scarce.

A single military engineer may simultaneously be responsible for:

- soldering and repairing Starlink terminals;
- modifying satellite communication systems for Starlink integration;
- integrating Raspberry Pi systems;
- building paired (“sparka”) energy systems for aerial reconnaissance posts;
- repairing ground robotic systems;
- handling continuous small-scale technical repairs.

Meanwhile, these engineers are also operating under frontline conditions with severe fatigue, sleep deprivation, and constant operational pressure.

This creates a critical bottleneck.

---

# Project Goal

## Build a Distributed Civilian Engineering Network

The objective is to create a reliable network of people operating in civilian environments who can systematically handle technical support tasks outside the battlefield.

Instead of concentrating all engineering work on exhausted frontline specialists, tasks can be distributed across multiple contributors working in safer and more stable conditions.

This enables:

- continuous production and repair workflows;
- specialization of labor;
- scalable technical support;
- reduced engineer burnout;
- faster deployment cycles;
- accumulation of technical knowledge and reusable solutions.

---

# Strategic Motivation

## Why This Matters

The frontline urgently requires:

- low-cost energy systems;
- repairable equipment;
- expendable power solutions;
- modular infrastructure;
- reusable components.

Commercial systems such as EcoFlow are highly effective but financially unsustainable for constant frontline losses.

Example:

This creates a strong need for:

- lower-cost alternatives;
- modular repairs;
- battery refurbishment;
- component reuse;
- field-adapted engineering solutions.

# Use Case: Extension Systems for Charging Stations

## Operational Problem

Charging stations for large “Vampire” batteries currently use extremely short:

- power cables;
- balancing cables.

Under combat conditions this becomes highly impractical and slows down operations.

Current inventory example:

- 6 charging stations of one connector type;
- 3 additional stations using different connector standards.

At present, manufacturing even a simple extension cable requires:

1. ordering components from multiple suppliers;
2. waiting for deliveries;
3. manually assembling each unit afterward.

---

## Immediate Deployment Potential

There is already an immediate operational demand for:

- 12 extension systems for power and balancing connections.

These would enter active use immediately after production.

A larger production batch (for example 20 units) could additionally support other drone units operating the same “Vampire” systems.

# 2. Repair and Refurbishment of Energy Equipment

## Objective

Create a structured repair pipeline for damaged or degraded field equipment, including portable power stations and improvised military energy systems.

---

## Example Scenario A — Electronic Warfare Power Box Failure

A mobile power box for EW (electronic warfare) systems stopped functioning during rapid relocation.

After disassembly, the issue was identified:

- several wires had been physically torn loose during movement.

The problem now requires:

- diagnostics;
- continuity testing;
- rewiring;
- system reconstruction.

This work distracts specialized engineers from higher-priority drone operations.

In other cases:

- batteries are fully degraded;
- components require replacement;
- systems must be rebuilt from salvaged parts.

---

## Example Scenario B — Reuse of Damaged EcoFlow Units

There are repair technicians purchasing damaged EcoFlow units that are considered economically nonviable to repair due to dead batteries.

These units still contain valuable reusable components:

- plastic enclosures;
- power management boards;
- ports;
- wiring systems;
- internal electronics.

Potential workflow:

1. acquire damaged units cheaply;
2. ship components to civilian engineering teams;
3. salvage reusable modules;
4. rebuild functioning systems from recovered hardware.

This significantly reduces production costs while increasing equipment sustainability.

# 3. Disposable / Recycled Power Banks for Infantry

## Concept Overview

There is significant demand for extremely simple low-cost power banks for infantry use.

The proposed model is based on reusing battery cells recovered from used electric bicycle batteries imported from countries such as Netherlands.

---

## Proposed Workflow

### Stage 1 — Acquisition

Obtain used electric bicycle battery packs from European partners and recycling channels.

### Stage 2 — Disassembly

Disassemble battery packs into individual cells.

### Stage 3 — Testing

Test and sort cells by:

- voltage stability;
- remaining capacity;
- discharge behavior;
- safety condition.

### Stage 4 — Assembly

Assemble low-cost field power banks using recovered cells.

---

# Frontline Use Case

## “Vampire” Logistics Operations

Every night, “Vampire” drone systems deliver supply packages to infantry positions.

Each package may include a power bank.

---

## Battlefield Reality

Commercial power banks cost approximately:

- 1400+ UAH minimum.

However, frontline usage patterns make them effectively disposable.

Typical lifecycle:

1. infantry charges radios or phones;
2. device is heavily used in harsh conditions;
3. power bank is lost, damaged, abandoned, or discarded.

---

## Existing Proof of Concept

A recent shipment from Dutch partners included:

- 47 reused power banks.

These were distributed directly to an infantry company.

---

## Operational Impact

For infantry units this creates:

- major cost savings;
- free access to basic power infrastructure;
- reduced dependence on expensive commercial electronics.

These systems are not perfect.

They fail more frequently than premium products.

But the most important factor is:

They work.

And in many frontline scenarios, a working expendable solution is more valuable than an expensive “ideal” one.

# Why This Project Is Realistic

## Battery Availability

The most expensive part of any portable energy system is usually the battery.

However, FPV units already generate large numbers of partially damaged or degraded batteries.

---

# Typical Scenario

### Example 1 — Training Damage

- new operators train at a range;
- drone crashes into a tree;
- battery becomes unsafe for high-current motor discharge.

The battery can no longer fly drones.

But it may still work perfectly for:

- charging electronics;
- powering USB systems;
- stationary energy storage.

---

### Example 2 — Charging Failure

- voltage instability during charging;
- one cell fails;
- battery becomes unusable for FPV flight.

Again, the battery is discarded despite still containing reusable cells.

---

# Reuse Potential

Such batteries can often be:

- disassembled;
- cleaned;
- repaired;
- reconfigured;
- repurposed for lower-power applications.

---

# Another Example — Obsolete Drone Systems

Military brigades sometimes purchase large batches of drones.

Over time:

- Russian forces adapt;
- radio frequencies become jammed;
- drones stop reaching targets.

The systems become tactically obsolete.

As a result:

These batteries could instead be repurposed into field energy systems.
